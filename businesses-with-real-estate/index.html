The Practice · 03

Business Sales & Acquisitions.

Owner-Operated · Asset & Share Sales · Confidential Marketing · With or Without Real Estate

By Taran Aujla, Salesperson

Owner-operated business sales and acquisitions, with or without their real estate. Asset sales and share sales. Plazas, gas stations and c-stores, industrial and flex buildings, specialty retail, small hospitality, professional service practices, and trade and distribution businesses. Files where the value sits in the operating business, the underlying real estate, or both. The mechanics that decide outcome are confidential marketing to the right buyer pool, lease and real estate review where applicable, the asset-versus-share decision, and a transaction process that holds together under scrutiny on both sides.

What this lane covers.

  • Owner-operated businesses sold together with the property they occupy, where the real estate and the operating business are both part of the transaction value.
  • Owner-operated business sales without the real estate, where the business is on leased premises or where the seller is retaining the real estate as a held position.
  • Acquisitions, on the buy-side, of operating businesses sourced through targeted outreach and confidential marketing rather than open listings.
  • Asset sales, where the buyer takes specified assets, licences, and goodwill, and assumes selected obligations.
  • Share sales, where the buyer takes the shares of the corporation that holds the business and its real estate, and inherits the legal entity.
  • Plazas, gas stations and c-stores, industrial and flex buildings, specialty retail, small hospitality, professional service practices, and trade and distribution businesses.

Who this is for.

  • Owner-operators preparing to exit and looking for confidential, pre-listing positioning before going to market.
  • Family-held businesses planning succession, sale to a strategic acquirer, or a transition out of an owner-operator model.
  • Acquirers running a buy-side mandate for a specific business profile, geographic footprint, or vertical.
  • Sellers and buyers who want the transaction structured so the substantive legal and tax decisions are handled by qualified professionals, not by the broker.

What the practice does in this lane.

The broker role in a business sale is the transaction role, not the structuring role. Substantive legal structuring, tax planning, and accounting treatment are handled by your lawyers and accountants. The broker role is to position the business, market it confidentially to the right buyer pool, find the right counterparty, and run the transaction process from listing or letter of intent through to close. The work the practice does ahead of the offer is what makes that process hold together when it gets to documentation.

  • Confidential positioning. Pre-listing review of the operating business and any associated real estate, with the marketing approach calibrated to the buyer pool. No open listings on the public boards where confidentiality matters.
  • Lease structure and estoppels where the real estate is leased. Lease term, renewal options, assignment rights, landlord consent, and the practical questions of what the lease looks like in the hands of a new operator.
  • Real estate considerations where the property is included. Title, environmental flags, lease estoppels on tenant occupancy of the property, and the closing mechanics where the real estate and the business close together.
  • Asset-versus-share decision. The position-level discussion of which structure works for each side, with the substantive tax and liability analysis routed to the client’s accountants and lawyers. The broker role is to surface the decision early, not to make it.
  • HST treatment. Where the transaction triggers HST, where exemptions apply, and how that affects the price agreed to.
  • Environmental flags. Gas stations, dry cleaners, industrial sites, and the practical question of what environmental diligence the lender and the lawyer will require, and how that fits the closing timeline.
  • Customer concentration and key-employee risk. What the business looks like to a buyer if the top customer concentration or the key operator leaves. What can be derisked through the structure of the deal.
  • Financials normalization. Working with the seller’s accountant to present financials in a form a buyer can underwrite, with add-backs and adjustments documented in a way that survives buyer due diligence.
  • Realistic buyer pool and process. Who the actual buyers are for this business, how they get reached, what their offer behaviour looks like, and what the negotiation will turn on.

Geographic focus.

The lane works most actively across the GTA and the Golden Horseshoe for plazas, industrial and flex buildings, specialty retail, and trade and distribution. Hamilton, Niagara, and Kitchener-Waterloo for owner-operated businesses with real estate, including small hospitality and gas stations and c-stores. Professional service practices across urban centres in southern Ontario where the buyer pool is regional or national rather than purely local.
