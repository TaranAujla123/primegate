Businesses sold with their real estate.

Businesses · Multi-Unit · Income · Cottage · Recreational

By Taran Aujla, Salesperson

Owner-operated businesses sold together with the property they occupy. The original transaction is two assets in one file: a going concern with its trade, equipment, goodwill, and operating history, and the underlying real estate that supports it. Both have to be valued, structured, and closed correctly for the deal to hold.

This lane also covers asset classes that pair with a held real estate component or function as a yielding asset on the books: multi-unit residential buildings, income-producing properties including short-term rentals, and cottage or recreational holdings with rental or development potential. The file mechanics that decide outcome carry across all of them: lease and rent roll quality, tenancy or operating regulation, tax structure, environmental flags, and the realistic cash flow on the other side.

What this lane covers.

Businesses sold with their real estate.

  • Plazas with anchor and in-line tenants, sold by the long-term landlord-operator.
  • Gas stations and c-stores, including branded and independent operations, with the underlying parcel.
  • Industrial and flex buildings owner-occupied by the operating business.
  • Specialty retail with established trade, brand, and customer base, sold to a continuing operator.
  • Small hospitality: restaurants, motels, boutique inns, country resorts, sold as going concerns with their real estate.

Multi-unit residential.

  • Purpose-built apartment buildings, small and mid-size, sold by long-term owners or operating groups.
  • Converted multi-unit and add-a-suite scenarios where the legal status and the actual rent roll need to be reconciled.
  • Mixed-use buildings with residential above retail or office at grade.

Income-producing properties, including short-term rentals.

  • Income properties held for cash flow, where the listed cap rate and the actual one are not always the same number.
  • Short-term rental holdings where the municipal regulatory environment can change the underlying economics quickly, and where the by-law trajectory is part of the file.
  • Single-tenant net-leased property and small commercial holdings on the books for yield.

Cottage and recreational holdings.

  • Cottage and recreational property with rental or future development potential, especially in Muskoka, the Kawarthas, and Georgian Bay.
  • Resort and lodge holdings small enough to be owner-operated, often paired with a small hospitality operation.
  • Larger recreational acreage where the use case combines hold, rent, and longer-horizon development.

Who this is for.

Two sides, often in the same conversation.

  • Sellers. Owner-operators approaching retirement or planning a structured exit, often holding both the operating business and the underlying real estate, sometimes through related corporations. Multi-unit owners and professional landlords exiting or rebalancing. Cottage and recreational owners ready to monetise or transition.
  • Buyers. Continuing operators expanding, family offices, multi-unit operators, holding companies seeking income property with an operating tenant or yield attached, and private capital with a defined acquisition thesis.

What the practice does in this lane.

Files in this lane are slower-moving and more technically complex than pure residential. The work that produces a clean closing is the work done at the front of the file: pricing the assets correctly, structuring the deal so each side's tax and legal exposure is known, and running diligence that catches the items that decide outcome.

  • Two-asset valuation, where it applies. Real estate priced against comparable commercial sales. Going concern priced on multiple of stabilised earnings, normalised owner compensation, and quality of the trade. The combined ask reconciled, not just summed.
  • Multi-unit operations review. Rent roll quality, tenancy compliance, fire and ESA status, work orders, capital deferred or pending, and the gap between the listed cap rate and the actual one.
  • Lease and estoppel structure. Where there are tenants other than the owner-operator, the lease terms, renewal rights, deposit treatment, and estoppel positions are reviewed and the gaps surfaced before the listing.
  • Rental regulation read. Municipal short-term-rental by-laws, residential tenancy law, condominium rules where they apply, and the practical trajectory of regulation in the specific market.
  • HST and tax treatment. Whether the deal is structured as an asset purchase, a share purchase, or a hybrid, with the tax implications mapped on both sides and the right professionals brought in early.
  • Environmental flags. Phase I and Phase II decisions, fuel-handling and underground-tank history on gas station and industrial files, dry-cleaning and printing residue on retail and industrial, septic and well-water regulation on cottage files, and the practical timeline for environmental clearance.
  • Confidentiality. Employees, suppliers, customers, tenants, and competitors should not learn of a pending sale until the seller chooses. The marketing, the diligence flow, and the offer process all run accordingly.
  • Buyer financing reality. Commercial financing structures, CMHC-insured options on multi-unit residential, vendor takeback structures, and the realistic credit profile of the buyer pool for the specific asset class.

Geographic focus.

Brampton, Mississauga, the Halton corridor, Hamilton, and Niagara for industrial, plaza, gas station, and multi-unit files. Cottage country and small hospitality across Muskoka, the Kawarthas, and Georgian Bay. Specialty retail and multi-unit residential across the GTA. Outside those geographies on the right file with the right counterparty.
